STYLE: Real live-action film footage, shot on grainy 35mm film stock in 1989 on location on a run-down residential street in Brooklyn, New York, during a heatwave. It looks like a gritty, low-budget independent film of that year: raw, real, slightly faded colour, heavy film grain, nothing polished. Hard, flat afternoon sun and deep shadows. No sun rays, no lens flare, no glow, no haze effects. The street is real and worn: cracked pavement, peeling paint, rusty fire escapes, black rubbish bags on the kerb, a battered old car. The colour comes from real things: red brick, painted doors, people's bright summer clothes. Every shot after the first is from very low down, almost on the ground, on a wide-angle lens held close to the fox, looking up at him with the brick buildings and fire escapes towering behind and above him, the frame tilted a little off level. The camera is locked off within each shot and cuts straight between shots. The stylized fox character from [Image1] and [Image2] is placed into this real world the way a computer-animated bear is placed into a live-action family film: his face stays exactly as the references, with realistic dense orange fur, damp from the heat, real contact shadows on the pavement. He is the only animated thing in the film. Everything and everyone around him is real, photographed for real.

PLACE: A Brooklyn block of red brick brownstones with high front stoops and iron railings. Real people sit out on the stoops in vests and shorts, sweating, fanning themselves with their hands, in the background. A plain white ice cream truck is parked at the kerb behind him, with no writing, no logos and no pictures on it. No readable signs, labels, graffiti or words anywhere.

CHARACTER - THE FOX: One fox, compact small frame, slim build, fluffy orange fur, cream cheeks, heavy-lidded golden yellow eyes, thick orange tail with a cream tip. For the heat he wears a plain white sleeveless vest, a plain red baseball cap with no logo, his two ears standing up through it, dark blue rolled jeans and white high-top trainers. Bored and grumpy in every shot: eyes alert under flat, heavy lids, brows a little low, mouth a flat line. His expression never changes, even when he wants the ice cream. Keep his exact face, fur colour and size the same in every shot. He has exactly two arms and two paws. He stands on the pavement in every shot.

PROP: One ice cream cone held upright in his right paw only, his left arm hanging at his side: a plain wafer cone about as long as his paw, with one perfectly round scoop of pale vanilla ice cream sitting on top, the scoop about the size of his fist. It is a round scoop, not soft serve, no swirl. It is firm and frozen and does not melt or drip at all. It is the only food in the film.

0-3s: High shot looking straight down from a rooftop over the whole Brooklyn block in the heat: the street, the stoops crowded with people sitting out, rubbish bags on the kerb, the plain white ice cream truck parked at the kerb, and far below, small, the fox in his red cap and white vest standing at the truck's hatch.

3-6s: Wide shot from low down at the side of the truck, looking up. A real man's arm reaches out of the truck's hatch and hands down the cone. The fox takes it in his right paw, his face bored and grumpy.

6-10s: Wide shot from right down on the pavement, the camera low and tilted, looking up past the cracked kerb at the brick buildings and fire escapes towering against a pale, hot sky. The fox walks slowly away from the truck straight towards the camera, the cone held up in his right paw, getting bigger and bigger in the frame, until he stops close to the camera, his body filling the lower half of the frame.

10-12s: Medium shot from low down looking up at him, tilted. He stands still and holds the cone up in front of his face in his right paw and looks at it under flat, heavy lids, bored and grumpy.

12-14.5s: Close-up from the side, his face in profile on the left of the frame and the cone on the right. He leans in slowly and puts out the tip of his tongue for one careful first lick, upwards on the side of the round scoop. The lick pushes the whole scoop off the top of the cone. It tips over backwards and drops out of the bottom of the frame.

14.5-16s: Close-up at ground level beside his white trainers on the cracked pavement. The round scoop lands with a soft splat and sits there, whole.

16-18s: Wide shot from low down, tilted. He has not moved. The tip of his tongue is still out, the empty cone still held up in his right paw, the scoop by his trainers, the people on the stoops behind him.

18-20s: Close-up of his face from the front, low down, tilted, the cap brim above his eyes, the empty cone in his paw below his chin. He pulls his tongue in and stares, flat and bored, straight into the lens. Nothing else moves.

SOUND: A hot city street in a heatwave: distant traffic, a car horn far off, people chatting on the stoops, the ice cream truck's engine idling. A soft splat when the scoop lands. MUSIC: an original late-1980s hip-hop beat with a heavy drum and bass line, playing loud from a radio somewhere on the block, never any existing song. The music cuts dead when the scoop hits the pavement and only the street sounds are left. No dialogue, no narration.

The ice cream is a round scoop, never soft serve, and it never melts or drips before it falls. He holds the cone in one paw only. No readable words, signs, labels, graffiti or logos anywhere, nothing written on the truck or the cap. No fire hydrant spraying water. No dog, no bird, no other animal. Only one scoop of ice cream, the size of his fist in every shot. No second fox, no change to his face, no feral animal on all fours, no human hands on the fox, no extra arms or paws, no distorted anatomy, no floating.
